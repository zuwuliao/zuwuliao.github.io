**LLM to AGI: Real Progress or Just Hype?**

I recently watched an interview with Andrey Karpathy that left me both intrigued and validated. If you haven’t seen it, I highly recommend checking it out [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXUZvyajciY&t=863s). In it, Karpathy—a well-known AI expert—makes a striking statement: “I did some AI consulting for computer vision. A lot of times, the value that I brought to the company was telling them *not* to use AI. ” I found that ironic, but not surprising. Many respected voices in AI, including Karpathy, Yann LeCun, and Richard Sutton, have expressed skepticism about the current trajectory of AI development, particularly with large language models (LLMs). I agree with them: LLMs are a technological marvel, but they are not a path toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). In fact, they might be leading us further away from it.

The more I study how LLMs are built and how they function, the more convinced I become that they are not advancing us toward AGI. Instead, they are simulating intelligence in ways that are impressive but fundamentally limited. Here are three reasons why:

**1. LLMs encode statistical patterns, not knowledge.**

Despite being trained on vast amounts of data, LLMs do not store knowledge in the way humans do. They do not retain facts, principles, or concepts as discrete units. Instead, they store statistical correlations between language patterns in the form of model weights. When an LLM generates output, it is not recalling knowledge; it is predicting the next likely word based on prior input. This means it can mimic understanding, but it does not *know* anything in the human sense. It cannot, for instance, create a new scientific theory or reason about a situation it hasn’t been statistically trained to handle.

**2. LLMs cannot revise their understanding.**

Humans continuously learn, adapt, and revise their beliefs. If we encounter information that contradicts what we previously thought, we can reflect and update our mental models. LLMs, however, cannot do this. Once trained, their weights are fixed. They do not have self-awareness, nor do they have a mechanism to track what they know or correct past "mistakes" in a meaningful way. Without the ability to revise or reassess, true understanding and learning are impossible.

**3. LLMs lack the diversity and uniqueness that fuel human intelligence.**

Human beings are individually unique, and this diversity fuels creativity and innovation. Different people interpret the same information in different ways, which leads to new perspectives, debates, and discoveries. LLMs do not have this variability. Models trained on the same data with the same architecture and hyperparameters are essentially copies. They do not have individual experiences or subjective points of view. Without diversity of thought, there can be no true innovation.

---

It’s easy to get caught up in the excitement around AI, especially when the outputs seem so coherent and intelligent. But we need to remember that coherence is not cognition. LLMs are powerful tools for certain applications, but they are not the thinking machines that many believe will lead us to AGI. If anything, their limitations highlight how far we still have to go.

Rather than placing our hopes in language models, we should step back, reassess, and invest in approaches that push beyond statistical mimicry. AGI requires more than prediction; it requires understanding, adaptability, and creativity—qualities that no LLM, as currently built, can deliver.
