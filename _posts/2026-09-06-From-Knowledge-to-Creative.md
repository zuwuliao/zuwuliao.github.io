I recently listened to Zhang Xiaojun's [podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E7qB_p1D0Xk&t=6116s) conversation with Professor Zeng Ming, former Chief Strategy Officer of Alibaba and a long-time researcher of corporate strategy.

What I found most useful was not any single prediction, but the framework he uses to understand AI as a new technological era. He connects AI with previous general-purpose technologies such as electricity, the Internet, mobile computing, and cloud infrastructure, then asks what happens to products, companies, strategy, and human work when intelligence itself becomes widely available.

I feel it very useful to me to understand where we are and where we will head to at AI era, I summarize his key points below. 

---

# Zeng Ming's View of the AI Era

## 1. AI is moving from infrastructure to applications

Zeng describes major technological revolutions as moving through three broad stages:

1. **Infrastructure formation** — the technology becomes mature, standardized, and broadly usable.
2. **Application explosion** — many products and business models emerge on top of the infrastructure.
3. **Native applications** — new products appear that could not have existed in the previous paradigm.

He believes AI is moving from the first stage into the second.

The first stage is foundation intelligence becoming infrastructure. Once intelligence can be measured, priced, and consumed in standardized units such as tokens, it starts to resemble compute, storage, bandwidth, or electricity.

The second stage is the rise of **Agents**.

At the begining of AI era, Human use a chatbot mainly answers questions, then human acts. With Agents, human defineds objectives, agents can plans, uses tools, acts, observes results, adjusts, then gets outcome that human wants.


This is why Zeng sees Agents as the new application layer of the AI era.

---

## 2. Foundation models become infrastructure

Zeng argues that model companies will increasingly resemble infrastructure providers.

As models improve:

- intelligence becomes cheaper;
- capabilities become more standardized;
- multiple providers become partially substitutable;
- application companies can call intelligence instead of building everything themselves.

This is similar to cloud computing. Most companies no longer build their own data centers or processors; they consume infrastructure as a service.

In this view, the model layer becomes something applications can rely on. Applications calls agents, and agents calls models, then comsume the result the model generates.

---

## 3. The important Agent is one that owns complex work

Zeng is skeptical of AI applications that automate only small tasks.

A feature such as, Generate an email, is easy for a stronger foundation model to absorb.

A much more defensible Agent would own a complete workflow like: Research customer, Decide next action, Contact customer, Observe response, Follow up, Evaluate outcome

The more an Agent participates in the full loop between decision and outcome, the more opportunities it has to improve.

That leads to one of the most important concepts in the interview: the **intelligence flywheel**.

---

## 4. The data flywheel upgrades into an intelligence flywheel

The Internet era produced powerful data flywheels:

More users → More data → Better prediction → Better product → More users


The AI era adds another layer. When an Agent actually performs work, it generates real-world experience:

Task → Reasoning → Action → Outcome → Feedback → Learning → Better capability

The system is no longer only collecting data. It is accumulating experience that can improve planning, memory, tool use, task decomposition, and judgment. That is what Zeng calls **intelligence compounding**. 

A simple way to express the difference is:

Data flywheel:
More data → better prediction

Intelligence flywheel:
More experience → better capability

More importantly, I think his idea of the **intelligence flywheel** can be extended further: the most important competitive advantage in the AI era may come from the ability to generate new data, with recursive self-improving, create better capability, then generate new knowledge, and use that new knowledge to create greater intelligence.

---

## 5. Organizations move from positions to tasks and company may disappear

Another important Zeng thesis is that the traditional company was designed around fixed positions and hierarchy.

The old model of company consists of:

Position → Employee → Manager → Department → Workflow

An AI-native organization may instead organize around tasks: 

Objective, Tasks, Required capabilities, Humans + Agents, Outcome

This reduces the need for some layers of coordination and approval. 

Even more, company as a result of Industrial Revolution works well in Industrial, Eletricity, Internet era, but may not fit the AI era. In this new era, Agents are the workforce. Company as an orgnization to get the job done will disappear. New form of orgnization might be created and take over.

---

## 6. Strategy moves from planning to generation

Zeng also argues that traditional strategic planning becomes less effective in an environment where technology and competition change rapidly.

Instead of Predict future, Make planm, Execute plan, companies need a continuous strategy-generation system:

Signals → Insights → Hypotheses → Experiments → Evidence → Decisions → New signals

The organization itself must continuously generate better strategic judgment.

---

## 7. Human value shifts from knowledge to creativity

Zeng argues that AI reduces the value of simply possessing existing knowledge.

If AI can absorb and reason over a large portion of human knowledge, then knowing more facts or procedures becomes less differentiating.

Human value shifts toward creativity, judgment, initiative, and the ability to create something new.

I strongly agree his this point. I will expand it in a littler bit.

# My Opinion — Agree, Disagree, Extend, and Challenge

**Agree.** What I like most about Zeng Ming's view is that he puts the current AI wave into a much bigger picture. Instead of looking at AI as just another software trend, he compares it with earlier technology shifts such as electricity, the Internet, mobile, and cloud computing. That helped me think more clearly about where we are today. I agree with many of his main ideas: foundation models are becoming infrastructure, Agents are becoming the new application layer, organizations will become more task-driven and less hierarchical, and strategy will need to become much more continuous. I also agree that the role of humans will gradually move away from simply knowing more toward creativity, judgment, and the ability to define important problems. As AI becomes cheaper and easier to access, simply "having AI" will not be much of an advantage. The real difference will come from what we are able to do with it.

**Disagree.** There are a couple of places where I don't agree with Zeng. First, I don't believe companies will disappear. I can imagine traditional hierarchies becoming much flatter, job boundaries becoming less rigid, and humans working side by side with Agents around specific tasks. But a company does much more than organize employees. It provides ownership, governance, capital, contracts, accountability, intellectual property, reputation, and long-term direction. Those things are still needed. So my view is that the company will stay, but the way it operates will change dramatically. 
I also don't agree that model companies and application companies will necessarily become separate groups. Of cause new application only componies will appear. But model company can build great applications too. And an application company may eventually build its own models. In fact, the combination can be very powerful: better models lead to better applications, applications create more real-world usage and feedback, and that feedback can help improve the models again.

**Extend.** For a long time, companies have treated proprietary historical data as a moat. I think that will become less true over time. AI is getting very good at understanding existing information, so simply owning a large amount of old data may not be enough. What becomes more valuable is the ability to continuously create new data through real-world actions, experiments, and interactions. That new data can lead to new knowledge, and that new knowledge can then improve both humans and AI. So I see the evolution as moving from a data flywheel, to an intelligence flywheel, and eventually to a knowledge-creation flywheel: 

*ambition → new question → human + AI hypothesis → experiment → new data → new knowledge → greater intelligence*. 

This also changes how I think about the human role. In the past, a lot of our value came from learning and applying what was already known. In the AI era, I think more of our value will come from deciding what is worth exploring, asking questions that have not yet been answered, and using AI to help create knowledge that did not exist before. In the end, the strongest advantage may be very simple: **how fast can we learn something new that others do not yet know?**

**Challenge.** There is also a challenge that comes with this knowledge-creation flywheel: **how do we know that the new knowledge AI creates is actually correct, useful, and good for society?** We are already seeing AI generate large amounts of code, research, mathematical reasoning, scientific hypotheses, business plans, and investment analysis. It is also beginning to play a meaningful role in areas such as drug discovery and scientific research. Today, humans can still review much of this work, although even that is becoming harder as the volume and complexity grow. But what happens when AI starts producing discoveries that are beyond the understanding of most humans? Imagine an AI proposing a new theory of physics, discovering a new biological mechanism, or designing a medicine through a chain of reasoning that no individual scientist can fully follow. We cannot simply accept something because a powerful AI says it is true. At the same time, if humans can only accept discoveries that we already understand, we may limit AI's ability to take us beyond existing knowledge. So verification may become one of the biggest challenges of the AI era. We will need new ways to test AI-generated knowledge through independent models, experiments, reproducibility, peer review, real-world evidence, and human judgment. As AI becomes better at creating new knowledge, **our ability to verify and govern that knowledge may become just as important as our ability to generate it.**
